Lesson 1

Understanding interest rates

Interest is the price of using someone else's money for a period of time. It is usually quoted as an annual percentage, but the amount you actually pay depends on how the balance reduces.

Annual rate vs total cost

A 15% annual rate does not mean you pay 15% of the loan amount in total. Because you repay part of the balance each period, the interest applies to a shrinking amount. Over twelve months, a BDT 1,00,000 loan at 15% typically costs around BDT 8,300 in interest — not BDT 15,000.

The useful question is never "what is the rate?" on its own. It is "what will I have paid in total by the end?"

Illustrative teaching point

Why frequency matters

Paying weekly instead of monthly reduces the balance sooner, which slightly reduces total interest. The difference is usually small. Fees and charges, which are separate from interest, often matter far more.

Lesson 2

Planning loan repayments

A repayment plan should begin with the quietest week, not the busiest. If the instalment only works during a good month, it does not really work.

A simple three-step check

  1. Write down your smallest realistic weekly or monthly income over the last three months.
  2. Subtract essential costs: stock, rent, wages, transport, utilities.
  3. Compare what remains with the proposed instalment. If the instalment is close to the remainder, the plan has no room for a bad week.

Keep a buffer

Aim to set aside a small reserve each week. A buffer converts a slow week into a manageable one instead of a missed payment.

Lesson 3

Managing business cash flow

Profit and cash are not the same thing. A shop can be profitable on paper and still run out of money if payments arrive later than the bills.

The cash-cycle question

How many days pass between paying for stock and receiving the money from selling it? That number — the cash cycle — determines how much working capital the business needs to keep running.

Illustrative cash cycle for three business types
Business type Stock paid Cash received Cycle length
Grocery retailer Day 0 Day 2 – 5 Short
Tailoring workshop Day 0 Day 14 – 21 Medium
Small distributor Day 0 Day 30 – 45 Long

A longer cycle means more money is tied up at any moment, which is why distributors often need more working capital than their sales volume alone would suggest.

Lesson 4

Maintaining repayment discipline

Repayment discipline is mostly a routine problem, not a willpower problem. Make the payment automatic, visible and early.

  • Automate it. A scheduled transfer removes the need to remember.
  • Separate the money. Move the instalment aside as it arrives, rather than at the end of the week.
  • Record every payment. Keep the reference number and date for each instalment.
  • Act early on trouble. Contact the provider before the due date if a payment will be short, not after.
  • Never borrow to pay a previous instalment without understanding the total cost of doing so.
Lesson 5

Identifying borrowing risks

Most borrowing problems are visible before the signature. These are the warning signs worth checking:

Costs that appear later

Processing fees, insurance, "documentation" charges or mandatory savings deducted from the amount you receive.

Pressure to decide quickly

Urgency is a technique. A genuine offer survives a night's sleep and a second opinion.

No written schedule

If the repayment dates and amounts are not in writing, they are not agreed.

Borrowing to cover an existing instalment

Rolling one loan into another usually increases total cost and lengthens the commitment.

Lesson 6

Reading digital payment records

A digital payment record usually contains five things. Learning to read them takes a few minutes and prevents most disputes.

  1. Amount — the value of the payment, before or after deductions depending on the provider.
  2. Reference — a unique identifier used to trace the payment.
  3. Status — completed, pending or failed. Only "completed" means the money has arrived.
  4. Timestamp — when the payment was initiated and when it changed status.
  5. Settlement — when the collected amount becomes usable, and any deduction applied.

Pending is not paid

A pending payment can still fail. Treat the sale as complete only when the status reads completed and the settlement is visible.

Lesson 7

Avoiding digital financial scams

Digital finance scams are common in every market. They usually rely on urgency, authority and a request for a secret.

Common scam patterns and safe responses
Pattern What it looks like Safe response
Fake support call Someone claims your account is blocked and asks for an OTP. Hang up. No legitimate support agent needs your OTP.
Advance-fee loan A "lender" asks for a fee before releasing a loan. Never pay upfront to receive a loan.
Overpayment trick An "accidental" extra payment followed by a request to refund the difference. Verify the original payment in your own records first.
Fake payment screenshot A screenshot shows a transfer that never arrived. Trust only your own app's completed status.
Guaranteed returns An investment promising fixed profit with no risk. Walk away. Guaranteed returns do not exist.

Never share these

PIN, OTP, password, card security code, or a full card number. No genuine institution asks for them by phone, message or email — and this demonstration never asks for them at all.
